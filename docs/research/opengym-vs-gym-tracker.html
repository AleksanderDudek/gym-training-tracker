openGym vs GYM TRACKER — feature comparison

State as of 5 November 2026. The openGym figures come from its repository at 8fb98b1 (commit of 4 Nov 2026, release v1.4.4), the README, NOTICE, ROADMAP, CHANGELOG, docs/ and frontend/src, plus opengym.ch, the App Store listing and the GitHub API (all listed under Sources, with the date each was read). The GYM TRACKER figures come from this repository at 277c660 (v3.0.0): the README plus checks in src/. Parts of section 3 describe work decided this round and implemented on branch feat/katalog-cwiczen (spec docs/superpowers/specs/2026-11-05-katalog-cwiczen-design.md).

TL;DR

  • Both are free, ad-free strength trackers built around privacy, but the architecture is the opposite. openGym is a self-hosted client–server app (React + a small Node API, Docker) with native phone and watch apps and multi-device sync. GYM TRACKER is a static PWA with no backend for data; everything lives in the browser.
  • openGym wins on reach and platform: an exercise catalogue of 5,632 exercises with licensed animations, App Store + Android apps, Apple Watch / Wear OS / Amazfit apps, widgets and Live Activity, two-way Apple Health / Health Connect, sync, self-hosting, 20 languages, imports from Strong / Hevy / FitNotes, plate math from the plates you own, and an open-source licence.
  • GYM TRACKER wins on coaching logic and breadth: an automatic progression engine that measures the starting level, an evidence-based plan advisor, 86 ready plan variants with deadlines, a coach app with end-to-end encryption (Sztab), gamification, and a whole nutrition side that openGym does not have at all.
  • The "same animation in every app" is Gym visual, a commercial stock library sold per item. openGym's licence covers openGym only. GYM TRACKER cannot reuse it; it takes the 1,324 MIT-licensed exercise texts from the original dataset and draws muscles with MuscleMap (MIT) outlines instead.

1. What openGym is

Positioning. "The gym tracker you host yourself": no account on someone else's server, no subscription, no ads, no telemetry; docker compose up and it runs. An in-browser demo with example data is at opengym.ch/demo.

Stack (README, docs/ARCHITECTURE.md):

  • frontend/: React 19 + Vite, React Router, Zustand. Training logic is pure functions in frontend/src/lib/ with tests beside them.
  • api/: plain node:http with three dependencies (@simplewebauthn/server, web-push, undici). Data is JSON under ./data (db.json, state-<user>.json); a database is the one planned compatibility break. OpenAPI spec in api/openapi.yaml.
  • web/: nginx serves the build and proxies /api so passkeys work on one origin.
  • Sync: one document per profile with a server revision; a stale write gets 409 and the current document; every field carries its own stamp, so the merge keeps the newest edit of each and deletions stay deleted.
  • Self-hosting: Docker images on GHCR and GitLab (amd64, arm64), Helm chart, Kubernetes manifests, a NixOS flake/module, guides for Cloudflare Tunnel, Caddy, Traefik, nginx, LAN HTTPS and a Mac with Tailscale.
  • Phone apps: the same code wrapped with Capacitor. iPhone/iPad on the App Store (with the Apple Watch app); Android as a signed APK in three sizes (Standard 180 px animations ~315 MB, Plus 240 px ~424 MB, HD 360 px ~760 MB); Google Play in closed testing; an F-Droid build without Google services planned for v1.4.5 (the animations marked as non-free assets).
  • Watches: Apple Watch (shipped); Wear OS (beta since v1.4.4); Zepp OS 3+ for Amazfit (v1.4.4, paired through your own server); Garmin Connect IQ (frontend/garmin, compiles and passes its checks, store listing and the phone bridge still to do).
  • Optional extras, off by default: AI coach with your own provider key, a local MCP server, signed webhooks (Home Assistant, n8n, ntfy). "Modules" switch whole areas of the app off.

Licence. Code under AGPL-3.0 with an additional permission (section 7) that allows distribution through app stores whose terms would otherwise conflict, as long as the source stays available under the AGPL. Contributors sign a CLA that lets openGym relicense their work. The exercise media are not covered by the AGPL (see section 2).

Features by area (README "Features", verified against frontend/src/lib/):

  • Planning: routine per weekday or a rotation; 4 starter plans; mini body map on each session; move a session without touching the plan; supersets, warm-up, drop sets, rest-pause, timed holds, cardio, rest per exercise, planned deloads; own exercises with photo/GIF/video (location data stripped).
  • Training: guided session with pre-filled weights, rest timer, live PRs; RIR/RPE; plate math from owned plates (plates.js) and per-gym inventories (gym-inventory.js, gym-auto.js); per-side reps; swipe a set left to delete (with Undo) or right to copy (use-swipe-row.js); pyramids; backup exercise per slot; machine stacks with odd steps; live session volume.
  • Progress: progression rules (linear, Greyskull LP, double, triple, time) that explain each target; e1RM curves; Structural Balance ratios (structuralBalance.js); activity heatmap; muscle map (volume, recovery, untrained); body weight vs goal; progress photos; lifts as % of body weight; edit any past workout.
  • Accounts and data: passkeys, pairing codes/QR, field-level merge; imports from FitNotes, Strong, Hevy (CSV or API key), Liftosaur/Liftoff, Apple Health weight; JSON and CSV export; plan sharing and PDF; optional admin dashboard and invite-only sign-up.
  • Phone and wrist: rest timer in the notification shade, reminders, Health Connect / Apple Health both ways, a geofenced gym reminder, Apple Watch app, iPhone widgets and Live Activity, AirPods Pro 3 heart rate on iOS 26, gym check-in QR/barcode card.

Languages. 20 per the README (English + 19 locale files in frontend/src/locales: ar, bn, de, es, fr, hi, hu, it, ko, pl, pt, pt-BR, ru, sv, th, tr, uk, zh, zh-TW); opengym.ch and the App Store say 19. The site says exercise instructions are translated in 12 languages and exercise names in 7.

2. Why "all these apps use the same exercise animation with muscles marked"

They buy the same stock media. The animated figure with the working muscles highlighted comes from Gym visual (gymvisual.com, © Aliaksandr Makatserchyk), a commercial library of exercise illustrations, GIFs and videos that markets itself to app developers, trainers and YouTube creators and adds new items weekly. openGym's README and NOTICE name it as the source of every still and animation in its catalogue. The MIT dataset hasaneyldrm/exercises-dataset, which redistributes ExerciseDB v1, puts "attribution": "© Gym visual — https://gymvisual.com/" on each of its 1,324 entries. ExerciseDB's own site does not name the source of its GIFs.

How Gym visual sells it (price rules page, read 5 Nov 2026), in USD, per item, per order:

Type 1–9 items 10+ items
Illustration$3.00$0.75
Animated GIF$3.60$0.90
Video (1–4 / 5+)$10$6

"Interested for a pack? Contact us." No pack or app-wide price is published. As arithmetic only, not a quote: 1,324 GIFs at the 10+ rate would be about $1,190.

What the licence allows (Non-Exclusive Commercial Royalty-Free License, "N-CRFL"): pay once, use in perpetuity, worldwide, including in Android and iOS apps and on websites. It is granted to one account owner (person or organisation) and is non-transferable. It does not allow reselling or redistributing the media, making it available for download, assigning rights to third parties, using it in products that "antagonize Gym visual business model", or training, prompting or generating content with AI.

What openGym's licence allows. From NOTICE.md and catalogue/media/NOTICE.md: the media is licensed to openGym only; the repository, the websites and self-hosted servers carry it at 180×180 px at most, larger only inside the app packages; there is no public API, CDN or media pack; "Third parties have no rights to reuse, extract, or integrate these media assets into independent apps, websites, or commercial products"; a non-commercial fork may keep the unmodified 180 px files with the notice, anything else needs its own licence; and the media "may not be used to train, prompt or feed any AI system". The MIT dataset has the same carve-out: its LICENSE file ends with a "MEDIA EXCEPTION" saying the MIT grant covers code, structure and instruction text/translations only, and the 180×180 media in images/ and videos/ is © Gym visual under Gym visual's terms.

So: the look repeats across apps because per-item prices are low enough for any app to buy the same set, and because free sources (ExerciseDB v1, the MIT dataset, openGym) ship 180 px copies with permission. That permission does not pass on to anyone downstream.

The small body maps are a different thing. The front and back figures with lit muscles that openGym shows on sessions, exercises and Stats come from MuscleMap by Melih Colpan (github.com/melihcolpan/MuscleMap, MIT): a SwiftUI SDK with male and female bodies, front and back views and 22 base muscle groups. openGym converted its Swift path data to JSON (frontend/src/lib/body-paths.js) and dropped the sub-group shapes.

3. What GYM TRACKER takes from openGym this round, and how

Decided and implemented this round (spec of 5 Nov 2026, branch feat/katalog-cwiczen).

(a) 1,324 MIT exercises, from the original source. Names, body part, equipment, target and secondary muscles, and step-by-step instructions in English and Polish, imported from hasaneyldrm/exercises-dataset at commit 7455efae41b330c265e7cd4b78dfa848e7ce5ebd (data/exercises.json, pinned and checked by SHA-256 in scripts/catalog/build.ts). Not from openGym's files: in openGym's copy the descriptions, muscle corrections and translations are AGPL. The dataset's LICENSE puts "instruction text/translations" under MIT, so its Polish steps can be used; all 1,324 entries have them (checked). The dataset has no Polish names, so the Polish names are GYM TRACKER's own translation. Plan in short: lazy-loaded data files outside the main bundle; ids kat-<id>; an exercise enters the progression engine only when added to a workout; the dataset's 21 targets and 39 secondary names map onto GYM TRACKER's 16 muscle groups, so catalogue exercises count in the advisor and "Muscles this week"; duplicates of library exercises point to the library version. Attribution and the MIT text go with the app.

(b) Muscle-map outlines from MuscleMap (MIT) at commit d53ddf4d480b65979f1008b203e6b6eda0e67e54: a front/back map with primary and secondary muscles on every exercise page, in GYM TRACKER's own colours, with the male or female body following the user's avatar. This gives the "highlighted muscles" effect people know from other apps without any Gym visual media.

(c) Not taken:

  • Gym visual media. Licensed to openGym only; GYM TRACKER would need its own paid licence, and the licence forbids redistribution and download. GYM TRACKER's repository is public, so committing purchased files to it is a question to put to Gym visual first.
  • openGym's 4,308 AGPL exercise texts and its Polish translations (catalogue/i18n/pl.json, 5,632 entries). GYM TRACKER's repository is public with no licence; importing AGPL text would oblige the whole app to be offered under the AGPL. That is an owner decision, left open.
  • openGym code. Same AGPL reason. Ideas below are re-implemented, not copied.

5. Analysis

Positioning. openGym is a strength log for people who want to own their server, with a well-built native shell and a large catalogue. GYM TRACKER is a coach in the browser: it decides loads, writes plans with deadlines and checks them against research, and adds nutrition. They compete for the same lifter, but openGym is closer to Strong/Hevy (log what you planned, see your numbers) and GYM TRACKER closer to a programme with a trainer.

Where openGym is structurally ahead (hard to close): native apps in the stores, watch apps that log sets, Live Activity and widgets; multi-device sync; 20 languages with a translation community; a 5,632-exercise catalogue with licensed media; an open licence and a large community (19.6k GitHub stars in under four months).

Where openGym is ahead but the gap is cheap to close: plate math, live PR flags, body map on session cards, edit past workouts, imports (ideas 1–5).

Where GYM TRACKER is ahead: automatic level finding and progression with test sets; the advisor; 86 ready plan variants (54 classic, 16 goal, 16 profile) with a calendar and grace windows; the Sztab coach app (openGym's trainer role is a roadmap item for February); nutrition, labs and the Polish food data; exercise snacks; gamification. openGym's own v1.4.7 "progression engine" and its AI coach aim at the first two.

Risks to GYM TRACKER's position:

  • openGym ships every one to two weeks and has a contributor base; the gap on progression and plans may narrow.
  • Without imports, people with years of Strong/Hevy history have no way in.
  • The 1,324 catalogue exercises have text and muscle maps but no animation; the gorilla mannequin covers only exercises with a movement pattern.

Caveats

  • openGym was not tested by hand (the demo was not opened). The openGym column reflects its repository, docs, site and App Store listing.
  • Languages: the README says 20, opengym.ch and the App Store 19.
  • opengym.ch contradicts itself on the App Store (the banner says it is there; the download section says it is "waiting for Apple's thumbs up"). The App Store listing exists (v1.4.4).
  • That ExerciseDB v1's GIFs are Gym visual is inferred from the MIT dataset's per-entry attribution and openGym's NOTICE; ExerciseDB's site does not say. "Many apps" use Gym visual is not counted, only shown by its own marketing and these datasets.
  • Gym visual pack and enterprise prices are not public.
  • The GYM TRACKER column is the repository at 277c660; the catalogue import and muscle map (section 3) were not in it yet.
